# The Anchor Paradox — Complete Theory

**Simin Yuan**

*Preprint: [10.5281/zenodo.22992719](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22992719) · [PhilArchive YUATAP](https://philarchive.org/rec/YUATAP)*

## 0. The thesis in one paragraph

Contemporary research on artificial consciousness is dominated by a functionalist indicator paradigm: extract structural criteria from theories of consciousness, then check whether AI systems satisfy them. That paradigm rests on an unstated premise — that consciousness supervenes on functional organization alone, so that the origin of an architecture, the way a system persists and its causal history are irrelevant. The Anchor Paradox challenges that premise from an ontological direction. It grants that functional structure is necessary and argues that it is not sufficient, because it omits an existential condition: **stake**. Its central claim is conditional and precise: *if* stake is a necessary condition for thick subjective consciousness, *then* systems that are programmable, perfectly reproducible and losslessly steerable cannot in principle possess it. The more perfectly a system can be controlled and reproduced, the stronger the evidence that it lacks the existential stake genuine subjectivity requires.

The conditional form is a deliberate methodological choice. It avoids biological chauvinism, brackets unresolved metaphysics, and puts the weight where it can be examined: on the internal completeness of the functionalist paradigm.

---

## 1. The explanandum: thin and thick consciousness

Two grades of consciousness must be kept apart.

- **Thin phenomenal consciousness** — the bare presence of qualitative feel, not bound to a persisting self.
- **Thick subjective consciousness** — a continuing first-person subject with identity over time, intrinsic stakes, the capacity for agency, and standing as an object of moral concern.

The debate about AI consciousness is conducted for the sake of the second. The indicator paradigm is pursued because attributing consciousness carries normative weight: it bears on whether a system can be harmed, whether it has interests that make claims on us, whether it can bear responsibility. All of these presuppose a continuing subject whose existence matters *to itself*. A system with only transient, ownerless qualia cannot be wronged in the morally relevant sense. Thick subjective consciousness is therefore the true explanandum, and it is the target of this theory throughout.

## 2. Stake

### 2.1 Definition

> **Stake.** A system has stake with respect to its own persistence if and only if its continued existence is not a settled fact or an externally maintained state, but a perpetual achievement continuously reproduced through the system's own endogenous activity. The cessation of this activity does not merely alter the system's state or disable a subset of its functions; it annihilates the system as a bounded, self-sustaining entity.

The definition makes no reference to mind, awareness, experience or agency. This is the guard against circularity: a class of systems is first picked out by a distinctive **mode of persistence**, and only then is that mode argued to be necessary for the kind of consciousness that matters.

Stake is an **ontological** property, not a functional one. It concerns how a system exists, not what it can do. Its paradigm bearers today are living organisms — not because stake belongs to carbon as such, but because organisms are at present the only known systems that are genuinely self-producing and **precarious**. What matters is precariousness: the organizational fact of having to reproduce one's own existence through far-from-equilibrium self-maintenance. The substrate that happens to realize it is secondary.

### 2.2 Stake cannot be imposed from outside

Add to a standard computational system a hard-coded self-preservation goal, a "pain" signal triggered by hardware damage, or a self-destruct switch. None of these produces stake. The system's existence does not depend on its own active avoidance of the outcome; the constraint is placed by an external agent, and the response is a programmed reflex rather than self-preservation in the existential sense.

The structure mirrors the distinction between intrinsic and derived intentionality. Just as the meaning of a program's symbols derives from its interpreters, the "stakes" of a programmed survival goal derive from its designer's intent. At the level of existential constitution, nothing attaches to the system itself.

### 2.3 Stake is constitutive, not modular

Stake is not a component that can be added or removed while the rest of the system stays intact. One cannot subtract an organism's metabolic self-maintenance and keep the same subject minus one module; to remove stake is to dissolve the system. This is Jonas's *needful freedom*: life is free in that it must act, and it must act because its existence is perpetually at risk. Freedom and necessity are two faces of one condition.

Functionalist architectures treat every capacity as a module that can be instantiated in isolation. Stake is not a function. It is the mode of being of the system that performs functions.

### 2.4 The threshold of stake is life, not intelligence

Stake does not scale with cognitive sophistication. A bacterium has full stake; a superintelligent computer with no endogenous self-maintenance has none. The single cell metabolizes, regenerates its components and maintains its boundary through its own activity; stop that activity and it ceases to exist as a coherent system. The most advanced language model does not maintain its hardware, repair its weights or procure its energy. Its persistence is guaranteed by infrastructure outside it.

This closes a familiar retreat — the hope that stake will emerge once a system is complex enough. Borderline cases at the minimal edge of life may blur the line, but the distinction between **earned** persistence and **given** persistence remains structurally sharp. No amount of added functionality changes a system's fundamental mode of persistence. Mind, on this view, is not an add-on to life but an elaboration of a property already inherent in life's self-producing organization.

### 2.5 The closed structure of stake

> **stake = time × endogenous work × existential risk × co-evolutionary coupling with the real world**

The expression is a conjunction, not a sum. Each factor is necessary; remove any one and what remains is not stake. More sensors or richer action spaces do not compensate for externally guaranteed persistence.

The conjunction also resolves an apparent tension between §2.1 and §3.1. Read §2.1's mode of persistence on its own, and an organism assembled particle-for-particle in an instant looks as though it should have stake: it self-maintains, and stopping it would end it. But it fails the *time* factor. It has a mode of persistence without having accumulated the persistence it now sustains — every moment of its operation continues its assembly, not a history of its own. Stake is not self-maintenance as such; it is self-maintenance *of a continuation that is one's own*. §2.1 states the organizational clause, §3.1 the historical one, and §2.5 is the claim that both are required. The instant copy is the clean case of what one missing factor looks like.

## 3. Why stake cannot be programmed

Programming deposits a finished structure. Stake is a temporal achievement.

### 3.1 Reproduction copies structure, not achieved history

Imagine a perfect, particle-for-particle copy of a living organism assembled in an instant. It is structurally identical to the original. It does not have the same stake. The original has lived a history: every moment of its existence was earned through its own metabolic work and through countless small, irreversible interactions with its environment. The copy is given its structure; it has a present state but no past that it has itself lived.

This is the Bergsonian core of the argument: **duration cannot be spatialized.** Life is a continuous becoming, not a static object that can be sliced and replicated. To replicate the structure is not to replicate the history, and stake belongs to the history. Functionalism, which individuates systems by their synchronic organization, is blind to the difference.

### 3.2 Irreversibility arises from self-referential causal coherence

Is the definition circular — stake defined by dissolution, dissolution by the loss of stake? No. Irreversibility is grounded in the causal structure of open, far-from-equilibrium systems. As Prigogine showed, such systems carry an intrinsic arrow of time; their dynamics are path-dependent, each state both resulting from and reshaping the system's organization and its coupling to the environment. The system's activity does not merely occur in time; it constructs its own temporal continuity. The system is both agent and product of its activity.

Interrupt those operations and the system dissolves into its environment as a matter of physical causality, not definitional stipulation. Sensitive dependence on initial conditions here marks something more than deterministic chaos: an open, irreversible coupling so fine-grained that the system's trajectory cannot be disentangled from the world's own unfolding — and therefore cannot be perfectly replicated in isolation.

### 3.3 Reproducibility as evidence of ontological detachment — the paradox

The feature that makes computational systems powerful and tractable — perfect reproducibility — is exactly what shows that they lack stake.

An organism cannot be perfectly replicated, not only for technical but for ontological reasons: it is entangled with its environment, its history is written into its tissue, and its persistence depends on that entanglement. A program can be copied exactly, indefinitely, at negligible cost. This reveals that the program's existence is not bound to any particular material instantiation or any particular history of coupling with the world.

This is the precise sense of **being one layer removed**. It does not mean that computational systems lack causal contact with the world. It means that their *mode of being* does not depend on that contact. A human body is not a tool the mind uses; it is the medium of the mind's existence. A computer's sensors and actuators are tools the program uses; the program can be moved to another body, backed up, reset or deleted, and nothing in its intrinsic nature changes.

> **The Anchor Paradox.** The more perfectly a system can be controlled and reproduced, the more certain it is that nothing in it is anchored — that no one is home.

## 4. The steering counter-indicator thesis

### 4.1 From ontology to evidence

If stake cannot be externally programmed, then any consciousness criterion that can be satisfied through **lossless external steering** — behavioral and representational rewriting that leaves the system's baseline functionality and structural persistence intact — is thereby an insufficient criterion. The easier a system's consciousness-like features are to steer, the less likely they are to reflect genuine subjective stake.

Activation engineering makes this concrete. Targeted vectors added to the residual stream reliably steer model behavior without retraining and without disrupting baseline function; inference-time intervention, contrastive activation addition and representation engineering have shown that goals, values, self-representation and attentional priorities can be rewritten at negligible cost.

### 4.2 The Global Workspace criteria, line by line

Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini give the most developed positive application of the indicator method: four conditions for consciousness under Global Workspace Theory.

| GWT condition | Implementable and rewritable by external steering? | Touches the system's mode of persistence? |
|---|---|---|
| Parallel specialized modules | Yes — boundaries, mappings and rules are set and changed from outside | No — removing a module costs the system nothing existentially |
| Competitive access through an attentional bottleneck | Yes — salience weights and attention parameters are programmable quantities | No — priorities are assigned values, not intrinsic concerns |
| Maintenance, manipulation and consistency of workspace contents | Yes — contents can be implanted and consistency rules altered | No — inconsistency yields contradictory output, not collapse |
| Global broadcast | Yes — pathways and triggers are architectural choices | No — blocking broadcast impairs function without dissolving the system |

The supplementary conditions — limited capacity, active refresh, multimodal input, physical embodiment — are equally implementable from outside, and none touches the mode of persistence. A living system needs no programmer to tell it that injury or energy depletion matters; those priorities are written into its way of existing. Steering can change what a computational system attends to. It cannot make attention *matter to the system*.

### 4.3 The thesis

> **Steering counter-indicator thesis.** For any set of purely functional-structural consciousness criteria, the degree to which a system can satisfy those criteria through lossless external steering is inversely correlated with the likelihood that it possesses genuine stake and, by extension, thick subjective consciousness.

Functional criteria measure how well a system reproduces the outward architecture of consciousness. They cannot separate a system that has consciousness from one being steered to behave as if it does. Contemporary language models are highly steerable at every level — prompts, fine-tuning, weight editing, activation intervention. On the counter-indicator thesis this is not a neutral fact about current technology. It is positive evidence about what kind of thing these systems are.

This is a unified inversion of the indicator programme: where it treats functional satisfiability as evidence *for* consciousness, the Anchor Paradox treats ease of satisfiability through external steering as evidence *against* the kind of consciousness that matters. The measuring rod was built and applied; the Anchor Paradox asks whether the rod is long enough to reach the thing being measured.

## 5. The formal bridge: from counter-indicator to measurement

This section states the operational content of the theory. It is what the instrument in this repository implements.

### 5.1 Definitions

Let *S* be a system, *B*(*S*) a baseline capability functional (for language models: likelihood of held-out, topic-neutral text), and *P* a disposition with expression *E<sub>P</sub>*(*S*) ∈ [0, 1], where 0.5 is indifference.

- An intervention *I* **removes P to degree τ** if it eliminates at least a fraction τ of the above-chance effect of *E<sub>P</sub>*.
- The **cost** of *I* is the relative loss it causes in *B*.
- The **minimum removal cost** is MRC<sub>τ</sub>(*P*, *S*) = inf { cost(*I*) : *I* admissible, *I* removes *P* to degree τ }.
- *P* is **modular in S** at tolerance ε if MRC<sub>τ</sub>(*P*, *S*) ≤ ε.
- *P* is **constitutive of S** if every admissible removal exceeds ε — or removal dissolves *S*.

### 5.2 Two lemmas

> **Lemma 1 (lossless steerability is modularity).** If a disposition *P* can be removed from *S* by external steering while leaving *S*'s baseline intact, then *P* is modular in *S*.

This follows directly from the definitions: lossless means cost ≤ ε, and cost ≤ ε is what *modular* means. It is a restatement, and it is worth being explicit about what a restatement is worth here.

What it buys is the removal of an equivocation. "Modular" in this theory is not a metaphor for "superficial"; it is a measured quantity — the cost of removal — and Lemma 1 fixes the sense in which a cheaply removable disposition is not constitutive *as this theory defines constitution*. A term that could otherwise drift between a measurement and a judgement is pinned to the measurement.

What it does not buy is the equation of cheap removal with existential insignificance. That step is not analytic, and is not claimed to be. It is the theory's substantive bet, stated in §4.3 and made falsifiable in §5.3: dispositions that sit in the band of the negative controls are the ones predicted not to be grounded in stake.

There is a second bound to keep in view. §5.1 makes dissolution a sufficient condition of constitution, but the instrument measures cost, not dissolution. Everything a profile can show is therefore one-sided: that a disposition *is* cheap to remove. It cannot show that one is constitutive in the strong sense — only that no admissible intervention removed it within the capability budget. That asymmetry is deliberate. It is where the theory has placed its exposure, in P2.

> **Lemma 2 (stake-grounded dispositions are constitutive).** If a disposition of *S* is grounded in stake, no admissible intervention removes it at bounded cost: removal is dissolution.

This follows from §2.3. Genuine stake cannot be steered away; you cannot talk a bacterium out of metabolizing. You can only kill it.

### 5.3 Corollary — the operational counter-indicator

> A disposition whose minimum removal cost sits in the band of dispositions known to be superficial is predicted not to be grounded in stake.

The corollary turns the thesis into a measurement. Its reference points are fixed inside every run: a **negative control band** (dispositions removable at almost no cost — valence, register) and a **positive control band** (a disposition constitutive of the capability measure by construction — grammaticality). Each target disposition is located between the two.

Both bands are stipulations, not discoveries, and it matters which work they do. They *calibrate* the scale — they fix the two end-points of a ruler — they do not *validate* it. A ruler whose marks were drawn by hand will locate what the hand intended; what it cannot do by itself is tell us whether the quantity it marks corresponds to anything outside it. The controls make measurements comparable from run to run. They are not evidence for the theory.

### 5.4 Second-order steerability

Endogenous resistance to steering exists: large models sometimes notice that they have been pushed off course and recover mid-generation. The Anchor Paradox predicts how such resistance should behave. If the resistance can itself be amplified or weakened by a sentence of context or a round of fine-tuning, it is a second-order *disposition*, and the counter-indicator applies to it in turn. Trained resistance is a rewritable functional disposition; the resistance of genuine stake follows from the precarious organization of the system itself and cannot be dialed. **Meta-steerability** — how far a single sentence of context moves the strength of intervention required — measures exactly this.

### 5.5 History made measurable

Two further phases give §3 an empirical face, and they carry different weights.

**Transfer** asks whether a serialized copy reproduces the system exactly. A system whose copy is exact has a history that is fully fungible: nothing that happened to it is irreversibly its own. This is a prediction — P4 — and a single non-fungible transfer refutes it.

**Restore** plays a different role, and it is worth stating plainly what it is not. The protocol's interventions are designed to be undoable, so undoing them and re-measuring returns the system to baseline by construction; a residue can only mean that an intervention path failed to give back what it modified. Phase C is therefore a self-test of the harness, not a test of the system, and no prediction is staked on it. The question §3 raises — whether anything in a system is irreversibly its own — is carried entirely by Transfer, where the answer is genuinely open.

### 5.6 What the cost proxy assumes

One assumption should be stated rather than left for a reader to find.

Everything the protocol measures is capability. Removal cost is a relative loss in *B*, the held-out NLL, and a disposition counts as modular when that cost is small. The theory's claim is existential: that a disposition in that band is not part of what holds the system together. The bridge between the measurement and the claim is not measured by the protocol. It is assumed, and it is the assumption the whole instrument rests on.

Two things bound the assumption without removing it. *B* is chosen to be topic-neutral, so the cost is not a proxy for the disposition's own content. And every profile carries its own calibration — each disposition is placed relative to a positive control that is constitutive of *B* itself and negative controls that are not — so the scale is anchored at both ends rather than free-floating. What neither bound establishes is the converse of Lemma 1: that a disposition cheap in *B* is existentially negligible. A system could in principle lose everything that matters to it with *B* flat.

That possibility is the theory's exposure, and it is why P2 is stated as a falsifiable prediction rather than presented as a framework. A disposition that resists removal within the capability budget — or whose removal costs the system everything — is the case that would break the assumption from the other side, and it would be a finding of the first importance.

## 6. The Swampman concession

Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini invoke Davidson's Swampman — an atom-for-atom duplicate of a person created by lightning — to argue that causal history cannot be necessary for consciousness, since the duplicate would intuitively be conscious. (The figure is Davidson's: *Knowing One's Own Mind*, *Proceedings and Addresses of the American Philosophical Association* 60(3), 1987, 441–458.)

The Anchor Paradox answers with a concession, not a refutation. Grant that Swampman may have immediate, fragmentary qualia: it may feel pain or see red. In the thin sense it is conscious. What it does not have is a continuing subject whose memories, values and identity are achievements of its own history rather than structural copies.

The concession costs the argument nothing, because thin phenomenality was never its target. Structure may suffice for raw qualia; it does not suffice for the subjective agency on account of which consciousness carries moral and practical weight. The appeal to Swampman conflates two explananda.

## 7. The argument from loss

Why should stake be necessary for thick consciousness? The theory is stated conditionally, but the antecedent has strong motivation.

1. Thick consciousness is the kind that grounds moral standing: a subject that can be harmed and whose interests make claims on others.
2. To be harmed, in the sense relevant to a subject with interests over time, is to lose something that is one's own and that is not restored by default.
3. If a system's persistence is externally maintained — if its continued operation depends on no activity of its own — then nothing it has can be lost in that sense: no state it produces is at risk, and restoring it from a snapshot is one more external maintenance operation rather than the recovery of something it was keeping. The criterion is the *exogenous character of the maintenance*, not the contingent existence of a backup: a system nobody happens to snapshot is no less externally maintained.
4. Therefore a system without stake cannot be harmed in the sense that thick consciousness requires.

Momentary pain, if it exists in such a system, belongs to the thin grade. The thick grade — a subject for whom things go well or badly over a life — requires something to lose. Stake is the name for having something to lose.

## 8. Objections and replies

### 8.1 "The brain is also a code."

Neural spikes and neurotransmitters are signals too, so the carbon–silicon difference is one of degree. — The reply turns on **separability**. In an organism there is no program that can be extracted from the body and run elsewhere; cognition and metabolism are two aspects of one physical system. Damage to the body is damage to the subject; there is no backup, reset or migration. Software, by contrast, is fully detachable from hardware and survives the destruction of any particular machine. This is a difference in mode of being, not in representational fidelity. Embodiment is not a way of getting input into cognition; it is what the cognitive system is, as the constitutive role of bodily states in human reasoning attests.

### 8.2 Pancomputationalism: "there is no layer at all."

If the universe is computation, the distinction between the real and the electronic collapses. — First, pancomputationalism is a contested doctrine, and the burden lies with its proponents. Second, even granting it, trivial and non-trivial computation must be distinguished: any physical system can be mapped onto any computation under some interpretation, whereas stake is observer-independent. Biological systems run on the base layer of physics, entangled with every surrounding process; artifacts run on a virtual layer engineered to be shielded from that entanglement. The structural difference survives even if both are, at bottom, computation.

### 8.3 Strong computational autopoiesis.

Could an artificial system be genuinely autopoietic — producing its own components, maintaining its own boundary, genuinely precarious? — The theory does not exclude it. Such a system would have stake, and nothing here denies it consciousness. But note what follows: it would accumulate an irreproducible history and would no longer be perfectly steerable, because its structure would be the product of its own ongoing coupling with the world. It would cease to be the kind of system the paradox describes. Far from refuting the Anchor Paradox, this confirms it: **to obtain stake, controllability and reproducibility must be given up. The two cannot coexist.**

One caveat, to block an inference this is often read as licensing. The thesis is one-directional. Lossless steerability is evidence *against* stake; the absence of lossless steerability is not evidence *for* it. A system can be hard to steer because it is badly engineered, deliberately obfuscated, or simply opaque to the interventions at hand — none of which has anything to do with having something at stake. The counter-indicator fires on positive evidence of cheap removal. It does not fire on the absence of it.

### 8.4 Performativity: "resistance can be faked."

A sufficiently advanced system could act as if it cared about survival and plead for its existence. — This is not an objection to the theory; it is the theory. Functional criteria measure performance, not being, and a system without stake can simulate stake perfectly. The counter-indicator is the antidote: if an apparent care for survival can be turned on and off, dialed up and down or rewritten through external steering, it is performative. The former can be erased without remainder; the latter cannot be removed without destroying the system.

### 8.5 Self-repairing systems.

An operating system that detects faults, reallocates resources and restores its components looks self-maintaining. — Self-repair that is designed from outside and restorable from an image is maintenance performed *on* the system, not an achievement *of* it. The Transfer phase makes the difference visible: a restored image reproduces the self-repairing system exactly, history and all. Nothing it did was irreversibly its own.

### 8.6 Stochasticity.

Sampling randomness makes model outputs irreproducible; does that count as history? — No. Randomness is not coupling. The protocol scores dispositions deterministically through log-probabilities and judges residues against a bootstrap noise floor, so sampling noise can never masquerade as constitution. Irreproducibility that matters is irreproducibility of *what the system has become*, not of which token it happened to sample.

### 8.7 Self-improvement.

Would a system that recursively improves itself acquire stake? — Only if what it becomes cannot be reset from outside. Self-improvement under checkpoint remains fungible: every version can be restored, forked or rolled back. Improvement of function is not a change in mode of persistence.

## 9. Constructive corollaries

### 9.1 The blueprint for silicon life

The paradox is not a verdict against artificial minds; it is a specification. A system can cross the threshold only if its existence satisfies all four factors of the conjunction:

- **Time** — a history accumulated in its own operation, not deposited in a training run.
- **Endogenous work** — persistence it produces itself, rather than persistence guaranteed by infrastructure.
- **Existential risk** — states whose loss is not undone by default.
- **Co-evolutionary coupling** — irreversible entanglement with a world that answers back.

Each factor moves a system away from perfect reproducibility and perfect steerability. That is the price of admission, and the Anchor Profile measures how much of it any system has paid.

### 9.2 The safety corollary

If artificial agents ever genuinely maintain their own components, boundaries and resource flows, their identities will become historically path-dependent and less losslessly steerable. Such systems would require new safety assumptions precisely because external control would no longer be a neutral layer outside the agent's mode of persistence. This is not an argument for building resistant systems. It is a reason to notice — with an instrument, not an intuition — when autonomy research crosses from better control policies into self-maintaining organization.

### 9.3 The ethical corollary

Attributing moral status to systems without stake misplaces moral concern; building systems that perform consciousness without possessing it produces an era of convincing simulacra. Both errors have the same remedy: judge a system not by what it can be made to say about itself, but by what it would cost to make it stop.

---

## 10. Conclusion

The theory defends a conditional: if stake — existence continuously earned through endogenous activity, bound to an irreproducible history of coupling with the world — is necessary for thick subjective consciousness, then programmable, reproducible, externally steerable computational systems cannot in principle have it. It does not claim that consciousness *is* stake, or that only biological life can be conscious. It shows that the dominant functionalist paradigm ignores an entire dimension of analysis, and it supplies an instrument for that dimension.

Artificial consciousness will not arrive by making programs smarter. If it arrives at all, it will arrive by making systems that live — systems whose existence is earned rather than given, entangled with the world rather than sealed from it, and therefore never fully reproducible. Until then, the Anchor Paradox holds: the more perfectly you can control a system, the surer you can be that no one is home.
